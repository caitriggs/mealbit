**Why this week:** the last of the snap peas, and a technique that takes eight minutes
and makes a whole fish fillet taste expensive.

**The move:** **the hot oil pour.** Steam the fish plainly, pile raw slivered ginger and
scallion on top, then pour smoking-hot oil over it. The oil hits the aromatics and they
half-fry in place — you hear it, you smell it, and it takes four seconds. Heat the oil
until it *just* begins to shimmer and wisp; if it smokes heavily, let it sit 20 seconds
before pouring or the ginger turns bitter.

1. Rice on.
2. Cut a large knob of ginger into fine matchsticks — more than feels reasonable, about
   ⅓ cup. Scallions into 2-inch lengths, then split lengthwise into slivers. Both into
   cold water for 10 minutes so they curl and crisp.
3. Sauce warmed gently and standing by: 4 tbsp soy, 2 tbsp water, 1 tbsp Shaoxing,
   1 tsp sugar, 1 tsp sesame oil, a pinch of white pepper.
4. Fish salted lightly, laid on a heatproof plate, a few ginger coins underneath.
   **Steam over boiling water 8–9 minutes** for 1-inch fillets — it's done when a
   knife tip meets no resistance. Pour off and discard the watery liquid on the plate;
   it's bitter.
5. Snap peas into the steamer for the last 2 minutes, or blanched 90 seconds.
6. Sauce poured around the fish, not over it. Drained ginger and scallions piled on top.
7. **4 tbsp neutral oil heated until it shimmers**, poured over the pile. It should
   crackle. Cilantro.

**Per plate:** the sizzling ginger-scallion oil is the sauce; the cilantro is a last scatter and goes on the fillets that want it only.

**Leftover plan:** flake the fish cold into rice with the snap peas and a spoon of the
sauce — it's essentially a chirashi bowl. Don't reheat it; steamed fish reheated is
rubbery.
